# Deliverable 03: Domain hierarchy and topic filter

Most commentary channels covering technology, finance, geopolitics, and cybersecurity split their own audience within ten uploads. One week they publish a semiconductor map, the next week they post a password-hygiene tutorial, and the week after they recap a central-bank press conference. YouTube's Browse recommendation engine tests each upload against the viewers of the last three videos. When fifty percent of the people who clicked a sovereign-debt explainer ignore a standalone cybersecurity video twelve days later, click-through rate collapses and Home-feed distribution stalls.

*The Reading Room* covers seven distinct subject areas across thirty flagship uploads between launch and January 2028, publishing roughly once every twelve days. Keeping all seven domains under one roof requires a strict gravitational rule: none of the seven subjects ever runs solo. Every episode is built as a three-layer collision modeled directly on Episode 01 (*The Allowance*), where an autonomous capability curve hits an institutional financial ledger and lands on a regular household's bank account, career, mental attention, or legal autonomy.

## 1. The three-layer gravity architecture

Every script, title-thumbnail pair, and research dossier in *The Reading Room Editorial OS* stacks its subject matter in the exact same order of operations. Viewers click for the bold technological shift at the front door, stay for the balance-sheet forensic audit in the middle acts, and subscribe because the final turn shows what laws have to change and how to protect their own household before the bill arrives.

### Layer 1: Frontier AI and autonomous systems (2027 to 2036 primary engine)

Layer 1 sits at the front of every package. It owns the thumbnail tension, the title premise, and the opening sixty to ninety seconds of narration. Every episode starts with a concrete capability curve unfolding between 2027 and 2036: coding agents compressing software engineering teams, robotic cranes clearing container ports through the night, five-gigawatt server campuses bidding for regional power plants, synthetic voice and video agents impersonating bank depositors, or autonomous cyber tooling probing industrial grids.

Episode 01 establishes this opening move in its very first sentence. Before touching historical labor strikes or constitutional court rulings, the script asks a blunt forward question: if software and robotics wipe out a massive share of W-2 paychecks before 2036, who pays off $40.5 trillion in US national debt? Within four paragraphs, it pins Elon Musk's October 29, 2026 X post and his July 2026 *Economist* interview in Texas to a specific calendar year, tracks the ninety-nine percent price drop in equivalent AI tokens since 2023 alongside Uber's $1,500 monthly per-engineer agent cap, and measures tech companies requesting five gigawatts on a single campus.

Without Layer 1 at the front door, an episode about Treasury auctions reads like dry bond-desk homework, and an episode about platform regulation reads like a law-review seminar. Layer 1 supplies velocity and generational urgency.

### Layer 2: Macroeconomics, sovereign debt, accounting, and market balance sheets (collision engine)

Silicon Valley whiteboards routinely assume physical infrastructure, thirty-year debt contracts, and federal tax collection will adjust to new software at zero cost. Layer 2 is the immovable wall those forecasts hit. Across Act I, Act II, and Act III, the script opens primary financial, fiscal, and accounting filings to test whether Layer 1's promise clears the books.

In Episode 01, Layer 2 carries the investigative spine through six verifiable balance-sheet mechanisms:

1. Federal tax plumbing from Table 9 of the Monthly Treasury Statement, showing individual income and payroll taxes supplying over 86 percent of federal revenue in fiscal 2026 while corporate taxes fell from 10.8 percent in fiscal 2024 down to 7.1 percent after the July 2025 tax law permanently restored 100 percent first-year write-offs for chips, servers, and domestic R&D.
2. Sovereign debt arithmetic from the Congressional Budget Office, where net interest passed $1 trillion in fiscal 2026 to beat the entire defense budget and climbs past $2.1 trillion a year by 2036.
3. Household liability lock-in alongside Irving Fisher's 1930s debt-deflation trap, pairing $19.1 trillion in American household debt (roughly seventy percent in fixed-rate mortgages) against shrinking human wage pricing power even under a two percent Federal Reserve inflation target.
4. Corporate cash-flow statements from SEC Form 10-K and 10-Q filings, tracking $740 billion to $820 billion in annual hyperscaler capital expenditure pushing Alphabet into back-to-back quarters of negative free cash flow and Amazon past negative $42 billion over twelve months.
5. Wealth concentration tables from the Federal Reserve Survey of Consumer Finances, proving the richest ten percent hold roughly ninety percent of stock market wealth while the bottom half owns one percent.
6. Wholesale physical pricing from PJM Interconnection's thirteen-state power capacity auction, where clearing prices jumped 833 percent from $28.92 to $269.92 per megawatt-day right before Microsoft locked up the restarted Unit 1 nuclear reactor at Three Mile Island for twenty years.

Any pitch that cannot name its Layer 2 ledgers by exact table, schedule, or statutory line fails editorial review before a single word of narration is drafted.

### Layer 3: Everyday life, cybersecurity, geopolitics, and tech policy (human and civic protection)

Data tables alone rarely convert casual viewers into long-term subscribers unless the math lands on a human life. Layer 3 threads through a named longitudinal character from Act I to Act IV, supplemented by the host's own stake as an eighteen-year-old who turns twenty-eight in 2036 right at peak career and mortgage-signing age. It answers two practical questions: what institutional rules have to change, and how an ordinary viewer protects their money, career, mental clarity, and personal autonomy over the next decade.

Layer 3 unites four civic and personal domains inside the channel's architecture:

1. Everyday household life and personal balance sheets. Episode 01 follows Claire, a thirty-two-year-old senior software engineer in Austin carrying a $3,400 fixed thirty-year mortgage until 2055 when coding agents force a seventy percent pay cut. Act IV translates the macro collision into concrete positioning: avoiding maximum thirty-year fixed debt that assumes Friday wages hold pricing power until 2056, and owning a direct stake in productive equity, land, or energy bottlenecks.
2. Cybersecurity and digital autonomy. When platforms propose Universal Basic Compute or closed-loop cloud wallets charging twenty percent exit fees to cash out to a bank, Episode 01 connects corporate cloud credits to Shenzhen's October 2020 digital yuan test where unspent balances vanished from phones after six days. Across the thirty-video slate, this pillar also covers synthetic identity wire fraud, automated vulnerability exploitation in CISA and NIST advisories, model-weight espionage, and household operational security.
3. Geopolitics and physical supply chokepoints. Software copies itself for pennies, while sovereign power sits inside copper transformers, Taiwan semiconductor fabs, subsea cables, low-tax intellectual-property routing through Ireland, foreign central banks holding US Treasuries, International Energy Agency forecasts of 945 terawatt-hours of global data-center load by 2030 (passing all of Japan), and West London M4 housing delays caused by server farms reserving local substations.
4. Tech and social-media policy. Every escape hatch runs into statutory law. Episode 01 tests citizen dividends against the 2017 Alaska Supreme Court decision in *Wielechowski v. State* after Governor Bill Walker vetoed half the Permanent Fund Dividend, pairs programmable compute wallets against Congress's 1938 Fair Labor Standards Act ban on company scrip after the 1912 Paint Creek coal strikes, and checks central-bank money printing against Britain's December 1, 1941 wartime point-rationing rules. Future episodes extend this statutory lens to algorithmic feed liability, youth attention capture, antitrust remedies, and financial fraud liability caps.

## 2. Hard kill-gates for pitch elimination

Before an episode concept enters the quantitative scoring rubric, it must clear four binary Pass/Fail kill-gates. Triggering even one Fail terminates the pitch immediately, regardless of how much short-term engagement the topic is generating on social feeds that morning.

### Kill-gate A: Perishable 2026 news gossip and executive drama

*The Reading Room* builds a thirty-video library engineered to pull Browse traffic through January 2028 and beyond. Pitches built around 48-hour executive feuds, leaked internal chat screenshots, congressional hearing soundbites, or weekly startup funding announcements fail Gate A automatically. Those topics lose ninety percent of their search and Browse velocity within six weeks.

Live news enters a script under one strict rule governed by Division 2's Hijack Radar (`news_to_thesis_radar.mjs`): a dated quote or filing may occupy two sentences in the cold open only when it exposes a ten-year structural contradiction. Episode 01 uses Musk's October 29, 2026 post as a six-second receipt before pivoting immediately to $40.5 trillion in federal debt and thirty-year household mortgages.

Whenever a dossier (`ep_04.md` through `ep_30.md`) cites a spot debt balance, yield, or capex figure that updates quarterly, the dossier wraps that number in a standardized `[LIVE_STAT_SLOT]` token refreshed on shoot week:

```yaml
live_stat_slot:
  slot_id: "MTS_TABLE9_CORP_SHARE_FY"
  fallback_baseline: "7.1 percent in fiscal 2026"
  primary_source: "US Treasury Monthly Treasury Statement, Table 9"
  refresh_window: "Within 72 hours prior to A-roll recording"
  thesis_break_condition: "Re-audit Act II if corporate share exceeds 13.0 percent"
```

### Kill-gate B: Single-model version hype and benchmark leaderboards

Any pitch centered on whether one frontier model beat a rival lab by four percentage points on a coding or math benchmark fails Gate B. Single-model version numbers age like milk, making a video look obsolete the morning the next checkpoint ships. *The Reading Room* examines unit economics and physical scaling laws across the entire capability curve, such as ninety-nine percent multi-year token deflation colliding with five-gigawatt substation queues.

### Kill-gate C: Backward-looking 2022 to 2024 tech retrospectives

Standalone history lessons dissecting FTX, the 2022 crypto lending freeze, Silicon Valley Bank's March 2023 weekend run, or early 2023 chatbot novelty fail Gate C. Dozens of channels already exhausted those post-mortems, and backward-looking nostalgia breaks our forward `2027-2036` promise. Historical episodes enter *The Reading Room* exclusively as empirical stress tests inside Act II or Act III to prove how an upcoming 2027 to 2036 escape hatch behaves under pressure, matching Episode 01's use of 1912 West Virginia coal scrip, 1941 British food ration points, and Alaska's 2016 dividend veto.

### Kill-gate D: Missing-layer amputation

A pitch must activate all three layers in one causal chain. If an idea has a clever SEC accounting anomaly (Layer 2) with zero Frontier AI capability shift (Layer 1) or zero household protection stake (Layer 3), it fails Gate D until the missing layer is locked with primary documentation.

## 4. The four official series playlists and algorithmic roof

On YouTube, a standard playlist used as a passive folder rarely extends viewing sessions. Following the `yt-series` architecture, *The Reading Room* organizes its entire thirty-video slate into four Official Series Playlists enabled via YouTube Studio's official series toggle. Marking a playlist as an Official Series instructs YouTube's watch-next sidebar to prioritize the next installment in our spine when a viewer finishes an episode, binding all seven topics into one continuous collaborative-filtering cluster.

Every series obeys four structural locks:

1. One master spine question big enough to sustain seven to eight standalone flagship episodes through January 2028.
2. Deliberate binge seams where the final sixty seconds of Episode N name the exact unsolved institutional tension that the next episode in the playlist opens on.
3. Shared visual packaging rules inside `reading-room-cinema` so thumbnails read as siblings in the watch-next rail while varying one focal object per episode.
4. Conversion-first entry doors where Playlist Position #1 holds the highest Subscribers-per-View standalone episode in that series, verified monthly in YouTube Studio Analytics.
